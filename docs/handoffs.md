# Patient Handoffs — Beginner Guide

**Education only. Never medical advice.** This page explains handoff basics for learning purposes. Always follow your facility's policy. If anything here conflicts with your facility's policy, your facility's policy governs.

## What a handoff is

A handoff is the transfer of responsibility and accountability for a patient from one healthcare worker (or team) to another — for example, at shift change, during a transfer to another unit, or when you step away for a break. A good handoff gives the receiving person the information they need to keep the patient safe.

## Why handoffs matter

Poor handoffs are a leading cause of preventable errors. Missing or unclear information about allergies, code status, recent changes, or pending tests can put a patient at risk. A structured handoff reduces the chance that something important gets forgotten.

## Common structured handoff tools

Many facilities use a standard format. Common examples include:

- **SBAR** — Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation
- **I-PASS** — Illness severity, Patient summary, Action list, Situation awareness and contingency planning, Synthesis by receiver
- **HEADSS** — often used for adolescent psychosocial screening (check your facility)

Your facility may require one specific format, a hybrid, or its own form. Learn which one your unit uses before you need it.

## What to include (typical checklist)

Not every item applies to every patient; follow your facility's required elements:

- Patient identifiers and room/bed (verify against policy; avoid unnecessary identifiers in notes you keep)
- Code status and isolation precautions
- Allergies (drug, food, environmental) and reaction type
- Current diagnosis and reason for admission/visit
- Recent vital signs and trends
- Pending tests, procedures, and consults
- Medications given recently and those due soon
- IV access, lines, drains, tubes, and devices
- Fall risk, skin/wound status, mobility level
- Diet, swallowing precautions, and intake/output
- Pain level and comfort measures
- Pending provider orders and pending results to follow up on
- Family/code status conversations and psychosocial notes
- Tasks remaining for the shift

## How to give a handoff

1. **Prepare before you speak.** Gather your report sheet, flowsheets, MAR, and any pending items.
2. **Find a quiet spot.** Minimize interruptions; ask to hold non-urgent pages/calls if your unit allows.
3. **Use your facility's format.** Stick to the structure so nothing gets skipped.
4. **Be concise and specific.** Say "fell out of bed at 0600, no injury reported" rather than "had a fall."
5. **Invite questions.** The receiver should repeat back key items (read-back) where your policy requires it.
6. **Document the handoff** per facility policy (time, who gave/received, format used).

## Receiving a handoff

- Listen without interrupting.
- Take notes on your own report sheet.
- Ask clarifying questions.
- Confirm read-backs where required.
- Note anything unclear and follow up with the outgoing worker before they leave.

## Privacy reminders

- Share patient information only with those involved in the patient's care.
- Avoid discussing patients in public areas (elevators, cafeteria, hallways).
- Follow HIPAA and your facility's privacy policy.
- Do not leave report sheets or printouts where others can see them; shred or dispose of them per policy.

## AI and handoffs (education only)

AI can help you *practice* organizing a handoff or draft a rehearsal script using made-up practice cases. Never paste real patient information into an AI tool. AI output must be reviewed against your facility's policy — it is not a substitute for clinical judgment.
